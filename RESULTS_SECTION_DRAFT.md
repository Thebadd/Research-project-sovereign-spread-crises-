We estimate the output response to a sovereign spread-crisis onset using local projections (Jordà, 2005), with the treatment defined narrowly: each episode contributes exactly one treated observation, its onset year, so that a crisis lasting five years identifies the coefficient exactly as much as one lasting a single year — the multi-year dynamics of an episode are traced out through the horizon of the outcome rather than through additional treated rows. This is a deliberate design choice with one open consequence worth flagging at the outset rather than at the end: episode duration itself is never used as a source of variation, so we cannot speak to whether protracted crises are systematically more costly than brief ones. All specifications include country and year fixed effects, a common set of predetermined controls — one lag of GDP growth, debt, the current account, banking-crisis exposure, government spending, openness, credit depth, and a hyperinflation flag, all measured the year before onset — and Driscoll-Kraay standard errors appropriate to a panel of this size. Horizons are labeled so that the crisis year itself is Year 1, following the convention of the reference literature; the pre-crisis baseline (Year 0) is by construction a fixed zero, and we return to the placebo point at Year $-1$ below.

Pooling all 61 identified episodes, the impulse response is negative and significant at the one- and two-year horizons (−2.3 percentage points, $p=0.014$; −3.0pp, $p=0.004$), weakens to marginal significance by Year 3, and is no longer distinguishable from zero by Years 4–5. On average, then, a spread-crisis onset is followed by a real but front-loaded output shortfall of two to three percentage points that fades within about three years. We treat this pooled average mainly as a motivating fact, because splitting the sample along the one dimension the literature suggests matters — how the crisis was resolved — reveals that the pooled number obscures the paper's central result rather than summarizing it.

Separating non-default from default-linked episodes and estimating both jointly against tranquil years, the two groups look almost nothing alike. The non-default coefficient is small, inconsistently signed, and significant at only a single horizon out of five. The default-linked coefficient is large — between −4.3 and −6.7 percentage points — and significant at every horizon from Year 1 through Year 5. What makes this more than an artifact of one group having noisier data than the other is that the formal test of equality between the two coefficients rejects at four of five horizons and is borderline at the fifth; the divergence is not simply a matter of one series clearing significance while the other does not. We take this as the paper's central finding: the output cost of a spread crisis is not a generic consequence of crossing the market-based threshold, but is concentrated in, and largely specific to, episodes that culminate in default.

A result this central invites scrutiny, and the placebo test we run on pre-crisis growth supplies a genuine reason for caution rather than a clean pass. Under valid identification, growth in the year before onset should carry no relationship to the crisis dummy; instead we find a significant *positive* coefficient for default-linked episodes specifically ($p=0.003$), with no such pattern for non-default ones. This is not evidence that default-linked countries were already declining before the crisis — if anything it looks like a boom preceding the bust — but it is a real departure from the null the identification strategy requires, and precisely because it falls on the same group carrying the paper's headline result, we did not treat it as a footnote to be acknowledged and set aside. One lag of GDP growth already sits in the baseline controls, so this particular pre-crisis window is mechanically absorbed by the main specification already; what remained untested was whether the boom extends further back than that single lag can see. Adding a second, non-overlapping growth lag to the exact headline specification moves the default-linked coefficient by no more than a tenth of a percentage point at any horizon, with significance preserved throughout. We read this as meaningfully strengthening the central result rather than merely qualifying it: the divergence between resolution types survives a direct stress test against the specific concern the placebo raised, even though it cannot rule out non-growth-related pre-existing differences we have no comparable way to test.

The two resolution groups are, of course, not exchangeable on observables to begin with, and addressing that selection produces a more complicated picture than either confirming or overturning the result outright. Reweighting by an estimated propensity score attenuates the pooled Act-1 cost from above rather than amplifying it — the unweighted average, if anything, somewhat overstates the typical cost once selection on pre-crisis fundamentals is accounted for. For the resolution split specifically, we follow the two-stage design used in the propensity-weighting literature more closely, scoring each type against tranquil years separately with its own weights; this confirms the extra cost of default at the one- and two-year horizons under both the unweighted and reweighted estimates, but the reweighted default-linked coefficient loses significance at Years 3–5, where the already-thin default-linked common support shrinks further under trimming. We do not smooth over this: the longer-horizon persistence that looks unambiguous in the raw joint specification is less certain once selection is addressed this way, The doubly-robust AIPW estimator, which we take as the preferred selection-corrected specification, tells the same story with sharper inference: estimating each resolution type against tranquil years and bootstrapping the *difference* between them on paired draws, the extra cost of default is significant at Years 1, 2 and 3 (roughly $-7$, $-10$ and $-7$ percentage points, each with a confidence interval excluding zero) and is not distinguishable from zero at Years 4 and 5. That two selection corrections differing in estimator, weighting and inference agree both on the short-horizon result and on precisely where it stops is what gives us confidence in the summary we adopt throughout: the resolution gap is well established at Years 1–3 and only suggestive at longer horizons. It is also the strongest single piece of evidence in the paper, since here the divergence between resolution types is tested directly as a difference, under an estimator that requires only one of the propensity and outcome models to be correctly specified.

Turning to mechanism, we replace GDP with six intermediate outcomes — private credit, bank claims on government, investment, government expenditure, the primary balance, and FDI — each estimated as its own local projection sharing only the onset dummy with the GDP specification. That shared structure matters for how the results should be read: finding that a channel moves significantly after an onset establishes co-movement, not mediation, since a common shock could drive both variables at once, or GDP could be driving the channel rather than the reverse, and we have tried to hold that line throughout rather than letting a significant coefficient slide into a causal-sounding sentence. Pooled across all episodes, private credit shows the clearest pattern — a response that builds from near zero at impact to a trough of roughly five to seven points by Years 3–4, echoing the delayed, compounding shape of the GDP result itself — while investment, government spending, the primary balance, and FDI each clear significance at no more than one horizon, and bank claims on government show no significant pooled response at all. Splitting these same six channels by resolution type is considerably noisier, resting on default-linked cells of thirteen to twenty-one episodes: of thirty resulting equality tests across channels and horizons, only two clear significance outright (private credit at Year 2, FDI at Year 4), with a couple of others borderline. Several point estimates in the default-linked column look economically large — credit and government spending both deepen substantially at later horizons — but because their equality tests do not reject, we read this as a power limitation rather than as an established difference, and resist building a "different transmission channel per resolution type" story on point estimates alone.

To get a sharper, more direct answer than parallel single-outcome regressions can give, we compute how much of the default-linked GDP coefficient each channel statistically absorbs once added as a control to the exact headline specification, on the identical estimation sample — a decomposition in the spirit of Gelbach (2016). Private credit and government expenditure each account for a rising share of the coefficient as the horizon lengthens, reaching roughly forty percent by Years 4–5; the primary balance and FDI account for essentially none of it at any horizon; investment sits in between, contributing a fairly stable fifteen to twenty percent throughout. This is, again, evidence of a statistical relationship rather than a causal one — a shrinking coefficient is equally consistent with reverse causality or a shared underlying shock — but it gives a genuine ranking among the six channels that the resolution-split comparison alone could not: credit and fiscal spending are the strongest candidates for further mechanism work, and the primary balance and FDI are not. We do not compute the same decomposition for non-default episodes, since there the GDP coefficient being decomposed is not itself distinguishable from zero, and a percentage computed relative to a number that is statistically indistinguishable from nothing is not informative.

The one piece of evidence in the paper that goes beyond co-movement toward an actual interaction design is a split of default-linked episodes by pre-crisis bank exposure to the sovereign — do banks that were already heavily invested in government debt behave differently once a default occurs than banks that were not? Here the mechanism evidence is genuinely sharp: in the high-exposure cell, bank claims on government rise significantly in three consecutive years following onset, a pattern entirely absent in the low-exposure cell and in non-default episodes regardless of exposure. The same split on the output cost itself shows a large gap in point estimates — roughly two and a half times deeper in the high-exposure cell — but that gap does not clear significance on its own, resting as it does on only seven treated country-years, so we present it as consistent with, rather than independent confirmation of, the reallocation story the claims data establishes more directly. The mirror-image finding for non-default crises is, if anything, the cleaner result: there, high pre-crisis exposure is associated with a cost statistically indistinguishable from zero, against a significant cost under low exposure. The formal difference between the two, however, is only marginal (a gap of roughly four percentage points whose interval just includes zero), and it moved across the conventional threshold between two bootstrap runs of identical code — on a cell of seven treated observations the resampling variance is of the same order as the gap being tested. We therefore rest this half of the result on the contrast in levels rather than on a tested difference, and note that no *difference* anywhere in the heterogeneity exercise clears significance on its own. Together these two halves suggest the sovereign-bank linkage cuts both ways depending on how the crisis resolves: protective when the debt is honored, and a channel for reallocation away from private lending when it is not — though given how thin the default-linked cells are, we regard this as the paper's most interesting result and its least statistically secure one at the same time, and would want it read that way rather than as a settled finding on equal footing with the central resolution-type divergence.
